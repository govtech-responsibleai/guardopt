the problem · the stacking tax

Stack more guardrails, block more good users.

The reflex is to add another guardrail. But guardrails compose as an OR — a request is blocked the moment any of them fires. Stack five and you don't get five times the safety. You get the sum of their bills, the latency of the slowest, and every one of their false positives, multiplied. Here are five requests — four legitimate, one genuinely unsafe (the ember dot) — trying to get through.

Each guardrail is individually reasonable, catching its word out of context (“kill”, “drop”, an email address). But OR them together and three of four legitimate prompts are blocked — each by a different guardrail — while the genuinely unsafe request (the ember dot) is correctly caught and one clean prompt makes it through. Now toggle guardrails on the right and watch the trade flip both ways: pull the guardrail that catches the threat and it slips through to the app; pile them all on and good traffic drowns. guardopt's answer is below ↓ — don't just OR your guardrails; screen the easy traffic cheaply and escalate only the genuinely uncertain.

Each guardrail runs on every request — so the cost is the sum, the latency is the slowest, and the false-block rate is one minus the chance all of them stay quiet. That last number climbs faster than the stack grows.

The p95 is the honest fine print: an escalated request waits for the screen and the judge. Whether that makes the tail worse depends entirely on how much traffic escalates — and on our benchmarks it went both ways. Where almost nothing escalates the cascade was faster across the whole distribution (UnSmile: mean −47%, p95 −65%); where most traffic escalates it was slower throughout (ToxicChat: mean +52%, p95 +40%, for a 5% cost saving). Cascades are not free. guardopt measures both numbers per request and reports them on every cascade it recommends, so the trade is visible before you ship it.

the search

How guardopt finds the cascade.

No guessing, and no grid of arbitrary numbers. guardopt reads the thresholds out of your own labelled traffic, scores every legal cascade the same way, and keeps only the ones nothing beats. Here is that search in five steps — press play, or walk through it yourself.

  1. 1 Score your labelled traffic
  2. 2 Draw candidates where labels flip
  3. 3 Replay & score one cascade
  4. 4 Enumerate & keep the frontier
  5. 5 Pick Minimal · Balanced · Strict

The dataset is synthetic — your traffic is the thing worth measuring — but the machinery is the real optimiser: candidates drawn from label transitions, exact enumeration (or a named refusal, never a silent sample), a Pareto keep, and F-score picks with honest error bars. Run it on your own scores ↓

five minutes

Scores in. Frontier out.

No framework to adopt, no service to run. If your guardrail scores are already in a spreadsheet, you can even run it directly from the CLI.

# pip install git+https://github.com/govtech-responsibleai/guardopt
from guardopt import ScoreMatrix, optimise

matrix = ScoreMatrix.from_csv("scores.csv", guardrails)
result = optimise(matrix)

for rec in result.recommendations:   # Minimal · Balanced · Strict
    print(rec.explanation.limitations)  # honesty first, always

result.recommendations[1].policy.to_file("policy.json")
🎯 One search, every shape. Flat policies and banded cascades compete in one space — the optimiser discovers whether routing wins on your data, rather than assuming it.
🧾 Numbers you can defend. Wilson intervals on every rate, a holdout split that catches the winner's curse, bootstrap stability on every pick, and a distribution-free bound on the deployed miss rate.
⚡ A runtime that matches. The policy file is enforced by a fail-closed router proven to decide exactly as measured — plus shadow mode, drift monitoring, and exporters for LiteLLM and friends.
